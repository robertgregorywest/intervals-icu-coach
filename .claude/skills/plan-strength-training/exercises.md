# Exercise library — for cyclists, by stimulus-to-fatigue ratio

Peden's catalogue, with the cyclist rationale, technique cues, and where each sits on the
stimulus-to-fatigue ratio (SFR). Select by **SFR for the current phase** ([periodization.md](periodization.md)),
not by what looks hardest. (Loads auto-regulated by RPE / velocity, never fixed kg — SKILL.md Constraints.)

Golden rule for selection: **"Stop asking what's the best exercise for cyclists. Start asking what's
the best exercise _right now_."** The best exercise isn't the most impressive or the most tiring —
it's the one that solves the problem in front of you at the lowest recovery cost.

## Bilateral primary (force ceiling)

High stimulus, **high fatigue** — the force-end builders. Full-range in off-season/build; shorten
range and cut volume in-season (see box/pin variants).

- **Back Squat** — the classic ceiling-builder, but **not mandatory.** "The goal isn't to get good
  at squatting, it's to get strong." If long legs, limited mobility, or old crash injuries stop you
  loading it well, **don't force it** — pick a variant below that lets you produce more force with
  better technique and less fatigue.
- **Box Squat / Pin Squat** — reduced-range, dead-stop squatting. Lower soreness, still a strong
  neuromuscular stimulus; the pause kills the stretch-reflex so you **accelerate hard** out of the
  bottom. A go-to in-season squat.
- **Leg Press** — big bilateral force with a stable, low-skill setup and low trunk/technical fatigue.
  Good when you want quad/whole-leg force without loading the spine or when technique limits the bar.
- **Trap-bar (hex-bar) Deadlift** — a hip-and-knee compound that's easy to load and accelerate;
  friendlier to the back than a conventional pull. Excellent for **max force and for jumps** (below).

## Posterior chain (the cyclist's under-built half)

Cyclists' quads are hammered weekly and are already the strongest part of the leg. Bias the
**posterior chain** — it's usually under-developed and it's where hip-extension force comes from.

- **Romanian Deadlift (RDL)** — _the first-reach hip hinge for cyclists._ Starting from the top and
  removing the floor makes it a **pure hip hinge**: shins near-vertical, minimal knee flexion, so
  load goes through **hamstrings, glutes, and spinal erectors** rather than the already-strong quads.
  Trains **hip-extension force in a lengthened position** and creates **less fatigue than heavy
  conventional deadlifts** (which also tend to get paired with squats — double fatigue). Conventional
  deadlifts still have value; the RDL is just the default tool for building resilient glutes/hams.
- **Seated Good Morning** — _"one of the best trunk exercises you're probably not doing."_ Sitting
  down removes most of the leg contribution vs the standing version, loading the **spinal erectors,
  glutes, and deep trunk** harder. Builds a **resilient, stiff trunk** plus **powerful hip
  extension** and hip-hinge control — a strong two-for-one (trunk + posterior chain) with good
  carryover to holding an aero position and transferring force through the hips.
- **Hip Thrust / glute bridge** — direct, loadable hip extension; strengthens glutes through the
  pattern that actually drives the pedal — the point of "get them stronger, don't just activate them."

## Single-leg (specificity + high SFR)

Don't dismiss single-leg work because you can't load it as heavy. Remove the second leg's support
and the working leg takes **significantly more than half** the load, plus it must stabilise alone —
demand per limb goes **up, not down.** Four reasons it's high-value for cyclists:

- **Specificity** — every pedal stroke is a single-leg action.
- **Cross-education** — training one limb drives neural adaptation in the other (why you keep
  training the healthy side through an injury).
- **Pelvic stability** — unilateral load forces the hip stabilisers and lateral chain to work, so
  you don't need endless banded walks for stability.
- **Lower spinal/systemic load** for a given leg stimulus → good SFR, especially in-season.

Exercises:

- **Step-up** — _Peden's favourite in-season exercise._ High force + single-leg specificity, with
  **light load and adjustable step height** to dial fatigue. **Technique:** stand close to the box;
  keep the **working leg planted**; slight **forward torso lean** (loads trunk + posterior chain);
  rise onto the **ball of the rear (support) foot** before stepping up, to **minimise push-off from
  the floor** — keeps it a true single-leg effort and puts you in a better position to produce
  **vertical force.** Low-box step-ups are a reduced-ROM in-season option.
- **Split Squat / Bulgarian Split Squat** — loaded single-leg strength through a big range; scalable
  by load and by how much the rear foot assists. **Contralateral loading** (weight on the opposite
  side) adds a pelvic-stability/anti-lateral-flexion demand.
- **Single-leg Leg Press** — heavy single-leg force with minimal balance tax when you want the limb
  stimulus without the stabilisation cost.

## Power / RFD (velocity end, low fatigue)

The low-cost way to train speed and keep the nervous system sharp — see principle 3 in
[principles.md](../plan-strength-training/principles.md). High neuromuscular stimulus, **low fatigue** → excellent in-season
and through a taper.

- **Loaded Jumps** (jump squat, trap-bar jump, dumbbell CMJ) — express force at **high velocity**,
  train RFD and fast-twitch recruitment for a fraction of a heavy barbell's fatigue. The tool to
  **keep the edge** when heavy lifting is too costly, e.g. deep in a race block.
- **Dead-stop / accelerative variants** — box squat, pin press, RDL-from-pins, pause step-up. The
  pause removes elastic assistance so you must **accelerate maximally** from a standstill —
  **directly specific to the pursuit standing start.**
- **Intent, always** — even non-jump lifts get maximal accelerative intent on the concentric.

## Trunk

- **Seated Good Morning** (above) is the headline loaded-trunk builder.
- **Anti-flexion / anti-rotation** holds and carries (e.g. loaded carries, Pallof) for a stiff trunk
  that transfers force through the hips and holds a fixed aero position under fatigue. Keep volume
  minimal — this is support work, not the session.

## Use with care / avoid

- **Leg Extensions** — isolation, knee-extension only; great for **hypertrophy or return-to-play
  rehab** (isolating a quad to rebuild a deficit), a **poor performance staple** otherwise. Cycling
  needs hips and knees working together — spend the recovery budget on compounds.
- **BOSU / unstable-surface training** — down-regulates force output (protective); limits strength,
  power, and transfer. Rehab tool, not a performance tool. Build stability from a **stable** base.
- **Band-only "activation" work** — fine as context/rehab/warm-up, but it won't make you stronger;
  if it's the _whole_ programme it caps your ceiling. Load hip extension to get strong.
- **Grinding heavy singles to failure in-season** — maximal work matters, but not all the time; the
  closer to failure and the more you grind, the more fatigue you buy. Save it for build phases.
