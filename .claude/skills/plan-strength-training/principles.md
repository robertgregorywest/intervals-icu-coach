# Principles — the Combined Athletic Performance method

The lens behind every exercise and set. Distilled from @chr1speden's posts; each principle notes the
idea and how it changes a decision.

## 1. Strength serves the bike

- Endurance performance is the goal. Strength and power are there to **support** it — the gym is a
  support system, not a competing stimulus. "If your gym work destroys the quality of your riding,
  you've missed the point entirely."
- **Aerobic work is always the higher priority** — even the strength coach says so. Never let a gym
  session degrade a key ride or track session.
- But strength and fitness are **built together, not traded off**. "Strength without fitness is a
  liability in endurance sport — and sometimes the other way round too." Being strong in the gym is
  worthless if you still get dropped; being fit but weak leaves force and durability on the table.
- The aim is **a stronger, more resilient cyclist — not a powerlifter who rides.** Judge everything
  by transfer to the bike, not by numbers on the bar.

## 2. Minimum effective dose · stimulus-to-fatigue ratio (SFR)

The single most important idea. Every exercise carries a **stimulus** (the adaptation) and a
**fatigue cost** (recovery debt). Select for the best ratio _for where you are in the year_.

- **"As much as necessary, not as much as possible."** The legs are already under chronic load from
  riding; the gym adds to the _same_ muscle groups. More gym work is not more adaptation.
- **Heavy, sluggish legs are a red flag, not a badge of honour** — a sign to be smarter with
  programming, not to push harder. The tell that it's working is often the _absence_ of soreness
  plus getting faster.
- **Volume is the first lever to cut.** Fewer exercises, fewer sets, fewer reps — executed with high
  quality, high intent, minimal form drop. Two crisp working sets often equal four grindy ones.
- **Coach by subtraction.** The hard part of programming isn't what to add — it's what to leave out.
  Ask "what can I _remove_ without compromising the outcome?" not "what else can I add?" Every
  accessory has a recovery cost; cut fluff.
- **Don't grind to failure in-season.** Proximity to failure is a fatigue multiplier. Auto-regulate.

## 3. Train both ends of the force–velocity curve · rate of force development (RFD)

Power output — the thing that drives the bike — needs stimulus from **both** ends of the curve.

- **Force end (heavy):** near-maximal loads build the ceiling — the upper limit of everything else.
  But heavy is slow by nature; at high loads velocity is always low, so heavy alone gives **zero
  speed stimulus** no matter how hard you intend to move the bar.
- **Velocity end (speed):** lower load, maximal intent, real bar velocity is where **speed** gets
  trained. This develops **RFD — how quickly you can express force** — down to how fast the nervous
  system recruits and signals muscle.
- **Why RFD matters on the bike:** reach peak torque **earlier in the pedal stroke** → go faster for
  less effort; and **accelerate / hit peak power sooner** when you attack — or **come off the gate**
  in a standing start. "Heavy builds the ceiling; speed trains how fast you reach it."
- **Loaded jumps** are the key low-cost velocity tool: express force at high velocity, train RFD and
  fast-twitch recruitment a heavy barbell can't, at **a fraction of the fatigue cost**. In intense
  blocks when heavy is too much, jumps **keep the nervous system sharp and fast-twitch firing.**
  "You don't lose strength overnight, but you lose neuromuscular sharpness fast."
- **Intent on every rep.** Whatever the load, try to **accelerate the bar maximally** — that's what
  recruits high-threshold units and trains RFD. If you grind everything, rethink your gym approach.
  **Dead-stop** variations (pause / from pins / from the floor) force hard acceleration and are
  especially useful in-season.

## 4. Exercise selection: multi-joint, stable, specific

- **Multi-joint / compound over isolation.** Cycling drives force through the whole kinetic chain,
  hips and knees together. Isolation (e.g. leg extensions) is for hypertrophy or rehab, not a
  performance staple — a poor use of an endurance athlete's limited recovery. → [exercises.md](exercises.md)
- **Stable over unstable.** BOSU / wobble surfaces down-regulate force production as a protective
  response — every rep is limited by balance, not force, so you get weaker and transfer little.
  Build a **strong, stable chain from foot to hip**: more stability → more force → more power →
  better performance. Instability tools are for rehab, not performance.
- **Strong, not "activated."** Glutes aren't "switched off" — they're always working; they need to
  get **stronger** through loaded hip extension, not endless banded abduction. Bands have a place in
  context, but if band work is the _whole_ programme you've set a ceiling on what you can become.
- **Specificity.** Every pedal stroke is a single-leg action — spend real time under load in that
  pattern (see single-leg work in [exercises.md](exercises.md)).

## 5. Individualise · evidence-informed, not evidence-blind

- **A system, not a template.** No folder of pre-written programmes. Start from the athlete in front
  of you: training age, race calendar, training load, injury history, movement quality,
  strengths/weaknesses. The right answer changes through the year. The question is never "what
  programme should I follow?" — it's **"what does this athlete need right now, and why?"**
- **Evidence-informed.** Research shows S&C benefits endurance performance and shapes the thinking,
  but an 8–16-week lab block under controlled conditions is not a year of managing a real athlete
  with travel, fatigue, illness, work and sleep. Research answers "can this work?"; coaching answers
  "is this appropriate for _this_ athlete, right now?"
- **Don't copy pro loads.** Live like a pro in the details (sleep, nutrition, recovery) and add only
  a small bump in volume; doubling load to mimic a WorldTour week ends in illness or injury.
